# What minimum sample, lag handling, autocorrelation correction and multiple-comparison control keep single-user habit-to-score insights honest, and how should effect size and uncertainty be shown without implying causation?
**Date:** 2026-11-04 · **Unblocks:** habit insight rules (issue #58)

## Answer
- **Minimum sample:** show an association only when each group (habit "yes" vs "no", or high vs low tertile) has at least 10 Nights, **and** the effective sample size after autocorrelation correction is at least 8 per group. Below that, show "Not enough data yet (n of 10)".
- **Lag:** pair each habit with a fixed, pre-declared target per habit, not a searched lag. Evening habits (alcohol, caffeine after noon, late meals) pair with that evening's Night (the Night dated the next morning). Daytime habits (water, macros, mood) pair with that Night too. Do not test multiple lags per habit; that multiplies comparisons.
- **Autocorrelation:** compute the lag-1 autocorrelation ρ of the score's residuals and shrink n to n_eff = n·(1−ρ)/(1+ρ) for the confidence interval (or use a block bootstrap with 7-day blocks). Also compare against the user's rolling Baseline (score minus 14-Night median) so slow drift and seasons don't masquerade as habit effects.
- **Multiple comparisons:** treat every habit × score pair evaluated in one refresh as one family; apply Benjamini–Hochberg at q = 0.10 and show only pairs that pass **and** whose difference exceeds a minimum meaningful size (e.g. at least half that metric's Spread, or 3 Sleep Score points). Everything else reads "No clear link".
- **Presentation:** lead with the difference in the score's own units and a plain-language range ("On nights after alcohol your HRV averaged 6 ms lower (likely between 2 and 10 ms lower), across 14 vs 31 nights"). Use "was linked with" / "tended to", never "caused" / "lowers"; add a fixed footnote that other things that happen on the same days can explain the link, and that the only way to know is to try changing one habit for a couple of weeks. No p-values or stars in the UI.

## Findings
- Multiple-comparison control: the false discovery rate procedure controls the expected share of false positives among reported findings and is more powerful than Bonferroni when many hypotheses are tested ([Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995, JRSS B 57(1):289–300](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1995.tb02031.x)). BH remains valid under positive dependence between tests, which applies to correlated scores (HRV, Sleeping HR) ([Benjamini & Yekutieli, 2001, Ann. Stat. 29(4):1165–1188](https://doi.org/10.1214/aos/1013699998)).
- Autocorrelation: positively autocorrelated observations carry less information than independent ones; under AR(1) the variance of the mean inflates by roughly (1+ρ)/(1−ρ), giving the effective-n formula above ([Bayley & Hammersley, 1946, JRSS Suppl. 8(2):184–197](https://doi.org/10.2307/2983560)). The N-of-1 methods literature flags serial correlation and carryover as the main threats to analysing single-person repeated measures ([Kravitz & Duan (eds.), AHRQ "Design and Implementation of N-of-1 Trials: A User's Guide", AHRQ Pub. No. 13(14)-EHC122-EF, Feb 2014; statistics in ch. 4, Schmid & Duan](https://www.aafp.org/content/dam/AAFP/documents/patient_care/nrn/design-implementation-n1-trials.pdf)).
- Reporting standards for N-of-1 work ask for effect estimates with confidence intervals, explicit handling of carryover and autocorrelation, and pre-specified outcomes ([Vohra et al., CENT 2015 statement, BMJ 350:h1738, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.h1738)).
- Observational self-tracking can only support causal claims under strong assumptions (no unmeasured time-varying confounding, correct lag structure); without randomisation, results should be framed as associations ([Daza, 2018, Methods Inf. Med. 57(1):e10–e21](https://doi.org/10.3414/ME16-02-0044)). The case for randomised one-person trials as the route to individual causal answers: [Schork, 2015, Nature 520:609–611](https://doi.org/10.1038/520609a).
- Lag for alcohol: in a large real-world sample (n≈4,098), alcohol intake reduced HRV and raised heart rate during the first hours of that same night's sleep, dose-dependently ([Pietilä et al., 2018, JMIR Mental Health 5(1):e23](https://doi.org/10.2196/mental.9519)). This supports the same-night pairing rule.
- Presentation: estimation (effect size + interval) is more informative and less misleading than significance tests ([Cumming, 2014, Psychol. Sci. 25(1):7–29](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613504966)); p-values do not measure effect size or importance and should not drive conclusions alone ([Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016, Am. Stat. 70(2):129–133](https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.2016.1154108)). Lay audiences understand uncertainty better in natural units and plain words than in probabilities or statistical jargon ([Spiegelhalter, Pearson & Short, 2011, Science 333:1393–1400](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1191181)).
- Consumer wearables (vendor documentation, not peer-reviewed). Oura "Discoveries" relate tags to biometrics from trends in past data. They need data from at least 14 of the past 30 days for a baseline, and they say correlations don't prove cause. No formula or significance threshold is published for personal Discoveries ([Oura Help Center, updated 2026-10-07](https://support.ouraring.com/hc/articles/30697762898323)). Oura holds a patent on tag-based insights ([US12288473B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12288473)); its claims weren't read. WHOOP Journal Insights compare yes vs no nights. Per WHOOP pages surfaced by search, they need at least 5 "yes" and 5 "no" entries within 90 days and are framed as association, not causation ([WHOOP, "A new way to see insights on which behaviors affect your recovery"](https://www.whoop.com/ca/en/thelocker/a-new-way-to-see-insights-on-which-behaviors-affect-your-recovery/)). That page returned 403 to direct fetch, so the details come from search snippets. Neither vendor publishes multiple-comparison or autocorrelation handling. Serenix's 10-per-group floor is stricter than WHOOP's 5/5.

## Open / uncertain
- The 10-per-group and n_eff ≥ 8 thresholds are engineering judgement, not taken from a paper; no source gives a universal minimum. Power is low at these sizes: only large effects (≳1 Spread) will surface. Simulate on the owner's real Nights before fixing the numbers.
- q = 0.10 vs 0.05 is a product trade-off (fewer misses vs fewer false insights); unvalidated.
- Oura and WHOOP publish only minimum-data rules and an association-not-causation caveat. Neither publishes statistics (test, lag, correction). WHOOP's 5/5-in-90-days rule is unverified by direct fetch (403).
- Confounding by co-occurring habits (alcohol with late bedtime, social days) is not handled by these rules; a regression adjusting for bedtime/Load could help but adds comparisons.
- Verified 2026-11-04: all 10 DOIs resolve in Crossref, and their titles, journals, volumes, pages and years match the citations. Cumming's article was published online Nov 2013 in the 2014 volume. Claims about paper contents rest on titles and abstracts as known, not full-text re-reads. The AHRQ guide's own wording on autocorrelation and carryover wasn't confirmed in full text. The original AHRQ link returned an empty page, so it was replaced with the AAFP-hosted PDF.
- Day-level habits logged after the Night (e.g. mood next morning) risk reverse direction; logging-time rules need a decision.
